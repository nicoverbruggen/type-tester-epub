Openers

The first page of a chapter has two jobs, Mr. Trevelyan liked to say, and neither of them is to be read. It has to tell you that something has ended and something else has started, and it has to do that before your eye has taken in a single word. A printer solved this centuries ago with an ornament and a large letter, and nobody has improved on it since, though a great many people have tried, mostly with rules and mostly on Tuesdays.

The ornament goes in the middle. Not because the middle is prettier, but because a mark that sits centred is read as decoration and a mark that sits at the margin is read as content, and the difference is entirely a matter of where it is.

The large letter is more delicate. Set it as an ordinary letter at four times the size and it takes part in its own line, which means the line grows to hold it and the line beneath is pushed away. The reader will not know why the opening two lines sit further apart than every pair that follows. They will only feel that the paragraph starts badly.

The remedy the trade settled on is to float the letter out of the line entirely, so the text wraps beside it and the spacing never notices. Tanya had a folder of books that did this correctly and a thicker folder of books that did not, and she had long ago stopped being surprised by which publishers appeared in which.

What she could not forgive was a book that got the ornament right and the letter wrong, or the other way about. It suggested that two different people had been in the file, and that neither of them had read the finished page.
